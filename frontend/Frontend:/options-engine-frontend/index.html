What it does

One function, five inputs, one number out. Everything else on the platform exists to get requests to that function and results back to people.

A European call option gives you the right to buy a share at a fixed price on a fixed date. For a plain contract like that, Black-Scholes gives the price in one line. For anything messier, there is no formula, and you have to simulate.

The engine does the simulation: it draws millions of possible futures for the share price, works out what the option would pay in each one, and averages. Because Black-Scholes exists for the simple case, we use it as an answer key. The reference contract (share at 1400, strike 1500, three months out, 6% rate, 30% volatility) is worth 52.6884. The simulation lands within 0.0044 of that at ten million paths.

All NSE-traded options are European-style, so this covers every real contract on the Indian exchange without any early-exercise machinery.

monte_carlo_call(S, K, T, r, v, n) -> double

S current share price
K strike price
T time to expiry, years
r risk-free rate
v volatility
n number of simulated paths

black_scholes_call(S, K, T, r, v) -> double
exact reference, used to validate the simulation

This is the whole contract between the C++ engine and the rest of the platform. The signature will not change even when the engine moves from a command-line binary to an HTTP service.

How a request moves

Pricing ten million paths takes real time, so requests go through a queue instead of blocking the page.

Submit

An analyst fills in the six inputs on the platform. The page shows the Black-Scholes reference price and an estimated run time before anything is sent.

Queue

The backend accepts the job, gives it an id, and puts it in line. The analyst can keep working; the job list updates on its own.

Simulate

The C++ engine picks up the job, splits the paths across all four cores, and runs. Each thread uses its own random seed and writes to its own result slot.

Report

The result is stored with its error bar, run time and delta, and shows up on the dashboard for risk managers to compare across jobs.

Benchmarks

Who uses it

Three roles, three different jobs to do. The platform shows each person only the controls they need.

Analyst

Prices contracts. Submits jobs, watches them run, reads results with their error bars and deltas.

  • Submit a pricing job
  • Track the queue
  • Compare against Black-Scholes

Risk manager

Reads the output, not the inputs. Looks at how prices and deltas move across jobs and whether accuracy is holding.

  • Dashboard and convergence charts
  • Job history and stored results
  • Engine timing trends

Admin

Keeps the thing running. Sets engine limits, manages who can log in, and sees everything the other two see.

  • Thread count and path caps
  • User accounts and roles
  • Queue controls
